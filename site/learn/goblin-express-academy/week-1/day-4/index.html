00 · Shift memo

Nobody gets paid for nothing

To:Courier Snag's handler (you) From:Madam Grizzletooth, Proprietor Re:Payroll

Snag has delivered a great many parcels this week and been paid for none of them. He hasn't noticed. Accounts has.

Starting today, jobs go up on a board and couriers pick their own. Good service earns tips, paid in whatever the customer has handy. Bad service earns a complaint, and I read every one. Three in one shift and I call you back to the Depot to start the shift again.

I'm told you have opinions about how all this should work. Good. Write them down. I don't honor verbal agreements, and neither will Claude tomorrow.

— G.

By the end of this module you will

  • Have Claude ask you the design questions before it builds, and make the calls yourself.
  • Keep the design in a file that you, Claude and a teammate can all check.
  • Build a big feature in slices, playing and committing each one.
  • Get Claude to do the math on your numbers before anyone plays them.
  • Check the finished game against the design, and settle any differences.

01 · The missing pieces · 10 min

A shift with nothing at stake

Snag can walk, dash and deliver, and customers rate every drop. But nothing rides on a shift yet: there's nothing to choose, nothing to earn and nothing to lose. Today fills in the rest of the loop from the game brief.

How many one-star reviews before a shift is cut short? Does Snag keep that shift's tips? Do far jobs pay more than near ones? None of these has a right answer in the code. Each has your answer, and you may not know it until someone asks.

Ask Claude for "a job board, tips and a fail state" and it will build all three. Along the way it will settle every one of those questions with a sensible guess, and you'll only find out which guesses weren't yours by playing. On Day 3 you put what you already knew in the brief (§10). Today you'll decide as you go, so turn it around and let Claude ask.

§11

Company policyLet Claude ask the questions. When a feature has choices only you can make, have Claude ask them before it builds, each with a suggested answer. Every question it asks is a guess it didn't make.

02 · Design it with Claude · 15 min

The interview

You're the designer. Claude reads the game, works out what it needs to know and asks. You answer, and the answers go in a file.

1. Start the shift

Terminal or PowerShell
cd goblin-express
claude

If Claude Code is still open from Day 3, type /clear instead for a fresh conversation. Leave it in ⏸ manual mode on for now.

2. Ask for the questions

Prompt
Today we finish the shift loop. Four things are missing:

- a job board, where Snag picks his next delivery
- tips, paid for good deliveries
- the shift ending early after too many 1-star reviews
- spending tips on upgrades between shifts

Read the game first. Then, before you change anything, ask me the design questions you need answered. Give each one a suggested answer, so I can just agree where I don't mind.

Claude reads the game, then asks. In our test run it asked fifteen questions, grouped by feature. Three of them:

From Claude
1. Where is the board? It could be a panel on screen, or a board tile in
   the depot that Snag walks up to.
   Suggested: a panel on screen. It appears whenever Snag isn't carrying
   anything, and you pick a job with 1 / 2 / 3.

8. How many 1-star reviews end the shift? In total, or in a row?
   Suggested: 3 in total during a shift. The HUD shows them as strikes
   (e.g. ✗✗·).

9. What does an early end look like, and do you keep that shift's coins?
   Suggested: a different title, "Sent home early", with a line in the
   boss's voice. You keep the coins you earned, because losing time is
   punishment enough.

Claude may show its questions as a menu instead, a few at a time. Pick an option, or choose Other and type your own answer.

Read every question, even the ones you'll agree with. A question you hadn't thought about is a decision Claude would otherwise have made quietly. This is where your experience counts: Claude knows how delivery games usually work, and you know how this one should feel.

3. Answer, and write it down

Agree where you don't mind, change what you care about, and ask for the design in a file in the same message:

Prompt
[your answers, for example: All your suggestions, except: call the money teeth, and drop the fourth upgrade.]

Write the design we agreed to DESIGN.md, in plain words a designer could check: the rules, the starting numbers, and what we're leaving out for now. Commit it on main, and don't build anything yet.

Why "don't build anything yet"? A full set of answers sounds like a go-ahead. When we tried it without that line, Claude started writing code the moment the answers arrived, before any design was written down or any plan for testing it in pieces.

4. Check what Claude filled in

Open DESIGN.md in any text editor and read it. Claude will also list details you never discussed, like how tips round or what the end screen says. Those are its guesses. Agree, or say what you want instead, and Claude will update the file.

That file is §5 at work: the design lives where tomorrow's Claude, and a teammate, can read it, and it's the yardstick you'll check the finished game against.

03 · Build it in slices · 35 min

One slice at a time

Three new features are too much to check in one go. Build them in slices, each one small, playable and committed before the next starts. It's Day 3's rhythm, applied to new features instead of a move.

1. Plan the slices

Prompt
Read DESIGN.md and split everything except the upgrades into about three slices I can play one at a time, smallest first. For each slice, tell me what I'll see and the quickest way to test it. Don't change any code yet.

Expect something like this: the early end first, because it's only a counter and an end screen; then tips; then the job board, the biggest piece. The upgrades wait for the side quest.

2. Build the first slice

Turn on accept edits as you did on Day 3: press Shift + Tab until the status bar shows ⏵⏵ accept edits on (on Windows, Alt + M if Shift + Tab does nothing). Then:

Prompt
Build the first slice. Then stop and tell me how to test it.

Claude will probably say it couldn't run the game. That's expected: it can't see your browser yet, so this week you're the tester. (Week 3 changes that.)

Cutting a shift short takes three bad deliveries, and a bad delivery means standing around holding a parcel. Use the quick test Claude suggests instead. It's usually a temporary change in the tuning file, like setting the limit to 1 so the first slow delivery ends the shift. Check the counter, the end screen and a fresh shift afterward, then put the number back and save the slice:

Prompt
Commit this slice.
§12

Company policyMake it quick to test. If checking a feature takes a whole shift, ask for a shortcut, like a lower limit in the tuning file. Put it back before you commit.

3. Tips, then do the math

Prompt
Build the next slice, then stop so I can test it.

Play a shift. Fast deliveries should pay the full tip, slow ones less or nothing, and a shift cut short should handle its tips the way DESIGN.md says. Commit it. Then, before you build the board, have Claude check the numbers you chose:

Prompt
Using the numbers in the tuning file, how many teeth would a careful player earn in one shift, and how many would a sloppy one? Show the math in a small table, and tell me if anything in DESIGN.md looks off. Don't change any code.
From Claude
Player                  Deliveries  Stars       Teeth per shift
Careful, dashing        ~11         5★          ~47
Careful, walking only   ~9          mostly 5★   ~35
Sloppy                  ~6          3★          ~9

When we ran it, the math found a real design flaw before the job board existed. The furthest jobs paid three times as much as the nearest for about a third more walking, so no player would ever take a near job. It also found that a sloppy player's first shift couldn't buy anything, and that a shift would rarely be cut short. Each of those would have taken many shifts of play to notice.

Decide what to change, if anything, and say so. For example:

Example
Go with your recommendation for the far jobs. Update DESIGN.md and the tuning file to match, then commit.
§13

Company policyDo the math before you play it. Numbers set by feel hide surprises. Have Claude work out what they mean for a careful player and a sloppy one, and fix the design before players have to find the problem for you.

4. The job board

Prompt
Build the next slice, then stop so I can test it.

Play it the way a player would. Does the board work the way DESIGN.md says? When you take a job, can you find its parcel? After a delivery, are there jobs to pick again? And is there a reason to pick one job over another? If there isn't, that's a design note for later, not a bug. Commit the slice.

5. Check the game against the design

The game was built in three pieces, with a design change in the middle. Time to check it still matches the file. Press Shift + Tab until the status bar shows ⏸ manual mode on, start fresh, and ask:

Prompt
/clear
Prompt
Compare the game with DESIGN.md. List anything that works differently from what the design says, and anything the game does that the design doesn't mention. Don't change anything yet.

When we ran it, this found three real differences, such as yellow arrows the design promised but the game hid while Snag was carrying a parcel, along with a list of small things the game does that the design never mentions. For each one, decide which is right, the game or the design, and say so:

Example
Fix 2 in the game. For everything else, the game is right: update DESIGN.md to match it.

Incident reports

Claude starts building while you're still deciding answers read as a go-ahead

Press Esc to stop it mid-step. Claude keeps whatever it has written so far, so send that back and write the design first:

Prompt
Put everything back to the last commit. Then write the design we agreed to DESIGN.md, and don't build anything yet.
The shift ends after one bad delivery a test shortcut got committed

A quick-test value is still in the tuning file.

Prompt
Check the tuning file for any values we changed for testing, put them back to what DESIGN.md says, and commit.
Blank screen after a slice an error in the console

Same fix as Day 3: open the browser console, copy the red lines and paste them in. Day 3, step 4 has the shortcuts for each browser.

The game has a new feature that PLAYTEST.md doesn't mention the checklist fell behind
Prompt
Add today's features to PLAYTEST.md, keeping it short enough to play through in about three minutes.

6. Clock out

Prompt
Commit any remaining changes on main, then show me today's commits, one line each.

Expect five or six commits: the design, one per slice, and the fixes from your math and design checks. Each one is a save point you can send back on its own.

04 · Checkpoint · 5 min

Sign the timesheet

Tick these off. They're saved in this browser only.

Employee of the Month

Locked. Finish the checklist to earn it.

Debrief questions

  • Which of Claude's questions hadn't you thought about, and what would it have built if it hadn't asked?
  • What did the math check find that would have taken many shifts of play to notice?
  • Where did the game and DESIGN.md disagree, and how did you decide which one was right?

★ Side quest · optional · ~30 min

Open the shop

DESIGN.md already says what Snag spends his tips on. Build it as one more slice, in a fresh conversation, and see whether the file alone is enough for Claude to get it right:

Prompt
Build the upgrades from DESIGN.md as one more slice, then stop and tell me how to test it.

Then do the math again, now that tips have somewhere to go:

Prompt
How many shifts would it take a careful player to buy every upgrade? Would any of them make the game too easy? Show the math. Don't change any code.

Or take the interview to your own game. Pick the next piece it's missing and let Claude ask:

Prompt
The next feature I want is [describe it in a sentence or two]. Read the game first. Then, before you change anything, ask me the design questions you need answered, with a suggested answer for each.
